# Findings so far

Part of the [shape exploration](README.md). Written 2026-10-02, after the pool was mapped ([04-assess.md](04-assess.md))
and nine small indie successes were placed on the same axes ([map.html](map.html),
[reference-games.md](reference-games.md)).

Each finding carries one of three grades:

| Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Confident** | stated by a source, or a plain observation that doesn't depend on how Claude cut or placed things |
| **Likely** | a pattern visible in the material and supported from a second side, but resting on Claude's placements or on a reading of intent |
| **Assumption** | an inference nobody has checked; treat it as a question |

Nothing here is a decision about the game.

## The main finding: core and supporting loops are spaced on purpose

**The user's finding (2026-10-02):** core and supporting loops "are spaced on purpose, they are part of the
tension/release cycle, but it's more like a swing between pillars, so that the player can take a rest from the
difficulty of each pillar in the other."

**Grade: likely.** The spacing is there to see; that it is on purpose, and that its job is rest, is a reading.

**What the maps show.** In seven of the nine games the core loop has a partner loop far from it on the
cerebral–kinetic axis: one is done with the hands, the other with the head. The gap is in axis units, where the whole
axis from Cerebral to Kinetic is 2.

| Game | One pillar | The other pillar | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 Minutes Till Dawn | move and shoot | pick an upgrade | 1.12 |
| Brotato | dodge while the weapons fire | shop between waves | 0.88 |
| Forager | gather | craft and build the base | 0.84 |
| A Short Hike | roam the island | collect golden feathers | 0.75 |
| Minami Lane | arrange the street | finish the missions | 0.72 |
| Kabuto Park | catch bugs | fill the collection | 0.68 |
| Islanders | place a building | end up with a pretty island | 0.66 |
| Stacklands | stack cards | feed everyone before the moon ends | 0.20 |
| SUMMERHOUSE | place pieces | soak in the mood | 0.28 |

**What supports the reading.**

- Guide [07](../../guides/game-design/07-keeping-players-engaged.md): a game has several pillars, each with its own
  intensity, and good pacing swaps between them before the player tires of one.
- The user's own observation on Noita ([brief.md](brief.md)): the tension–release cycle sits "not in difficulty, but
  viable choices. Sometimes you just shoot with your loadout, but sometimes you stop and design it."
- In Brotato and 20 Minutes Till Dawn the two pillars alternate on a fixed beat (a wave, then the shop; a stretch of
  dodging, then an upgrade), which is hard to explain as an accident.

**What limits it.**

- The positions, and so the gaps, are Claude's placements. The user checked the games they have played and found
  nothing to disagree with; the rest rests on that trust.
- No developer of these games was read saying the spacing was intended.
- **The two exceptions matter.** Stacklands, one of the best sellers on the list (30,383 reviews), has no swing in pace: every loop is
  thinking. SUMMERHOUSE has none either. So a swing between a fast and a slow pillar is common in this sample, not
  required. Stacklands may swing in another way (between calm stacking and the deadline of the moon), which this axis
  doesn't show.

## The user's leaning from this (2026-10-02, not a decision)

> "This tells me that I need to lean into the excitement part a bit, so that the player can take a break from the
> simulation / discovery part."

Claude's notes on it:

- **It matches the brief.** The user had already fixed "casual during play, with moments to stop and think"
  ([brief.md](brief.md)). The pool came out almost all thinking because the brainstorm collected ways to act on the
  simulation; the casual, hands-on pillar was never collected.
- **What the sample shows is a hands pillar, which is not the same as Excitement.** On Quantic Foundry's chart
  Excitement means fast-paced action and thrills. The peaceful games on the list get their swing from something
  milder: roaming (A Short Hike), gathering (Forager). These sit between 0.30 and 0.40 toward
  Kinetic, with no violence and no time pressure. So the leaning can be followed without leaving "peaceful" or the
  user's doubt about high-tension play.
- **Which pillar is the rest depends on the game.** In Brotato the shop is the rest from dodging. In a game whose
  demanding part is understanding a simulation, the hands pillar would be the rest.
- **Grade.** It follows from a finding graded "likely", and Stacklands shows a successful game without such a pillar.

## Confident

1. **Every game on the list has one dominant activity that can be named in a verb** (roam, place, stack, dodge, catch,
   gather). This holds however finely the games are cut into loops.
2. **Far apart on the map means independent, not opposed.** Quantic Foundry's own words: motivations that are farther
   apart "don't suppress each other". A game can serve two far-apart motivations.
3. **Games do appear under more than one motivation.** Animal Crossing and Pokémon are on both the Design list and the
   Completion list in Quantic Foundry's data, although those two are 2.36 squares apart on the map.
4. **Short to the first sale, long afterwards.** Brotato went on sale after 3 months and 20 Minutes Till Dawn after 2;
   both were then developed for about a year more in early access. Luck be a Landlord spent two years there.
5. **Small games have been made in the 4 to 8 month window and sold far above the "hit" line.** A Short Hike (4
   months, 22,778 reviews), Minami Lane (about 6, 6,786), Islanders (7, 16,787).
6. **One game on the list has a published cost.** Kabuto Park: 9 months, about €65,000, break-even near 30,000 copies,
   reached within the first month.
7. **A toy with no goals has sold.** SUMMERHOUSE: no score, no win, 3,382 reviews. One case.
8. **Two pairs on the list are repeat developers.** Minami Lane and Kabuto Park share a programmer; Islanders and
   SUMMERHOUSE share a designer.
9. **The list says nothing about modest success.** Every game on it is far above 1,000 reviews; the lowest has 3,382.
   The 200 to 300 review tier, which the user's Steam plan is judged by, is not represented.
10. **The model has no motivation for nurturing, cuteness or relaxing.** Shapes built on those can't be judged by the
    map.

## Likely

1. **Small successful games reach across the map.** All nine have loops on both sides of at least one axis (SUMMERHOUSE only through a side loop).
   Supported independently by point 3 above. How far they reach is not reliable: cutting a game into loops and
   placing each near the motivation it resembles spreads any game.
2. **Two halves can be one small game when one half is short and exists to feed the other.** Brotato and 20 Minutes
   Till Dawn pair a kinetic half with a thinking half and were built in two to three months. This bears on the user's
   worry that prep-and-run "might be TWO games".
3. **The peaceful games on the list sit where this game's pool sits.** A Short Hike, Minami Lane, SUMMERHOUSE and
   Kabuto Park are left of the vertical axis; Stacklands is almost entirely inside the pool's area. Both sets of
   positions are Claude's.
4. **This game's pool is almost all on the thinking side.** Of 36 marks, six are below the horizontal axis and none
   is further than 0.32 toward Kinetic. Read against the main finding: as it stands, the pool offers many slow pillars
   and hardly a fast one. The shapes that would supply one are the runs, the front and the character as centre.
5. **The toy is Discovery, and its two near neighbourhoods are far from each other** (Design on one side; Strategy and
   Completion on the other). The distances are measured on the picture; that the toy is Discovery is the user's own
   "surprise comes from discovery".
6. **Peaceful costs little on the map.** The only motivation the user's limit rules out is Destruction.

## Assumptions

1. **That a loop can be placed on a map made from players' motivations at all.** Everything on the map page rests on
   this. The user's check of familiar games is the only test so far.
2. **"One core, two supporting, one side."** The count comes from how finely Claude cut each game, which was a free
   choice. Only "one dominant activity" survives a different cut.
3. **The pool's two anchors, Power as an add-on and difficulty as a dial** ([03-consolidate.md](03-consolidate.md)).
   Claude's grouping; the user has not confirmed it.
4. **A wide mix widens the audience when its parts are optional to each other and narrows it when both are forced.**
   Inferred from how Quantic Foundry built its game lists; not stated by the source.
5. **That any of these patterns has to do with success.** All nine games are successes picked by Claude and the user.
   No failed game was placed, so nothing here separates what successful games do from what all games do.
6. **That the decline of the Strategy motivation touches light planning games.** The decline is sourced (strategy/09);
   the games behind the Strategy motivation are heavy ones, and whether a casual game with a little planning is
   affected is unknown.
7. **Exact distances and left–right positions.** Differences under about 0.1 on the axes, or 0.3 squares on the map,
   mean nothing. The World–Player position of a loop is the softest judgement of all.
8. **Development times that have no source:** SUMMERHOUSE, and Forager's "about two years". Stacklands is under 4
   months by the user's word (2026-10-02), with no written source on file.
9. **Time spent per loop.** Not estimated, on purpose. The user: it "depends on playstyle too much, and less so on the
   game itself".
