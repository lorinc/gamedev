# Creator outreach by cold email

Scope: how a solo dev with no budget gets YouTubers to play the game, and what that's worth. General, for any game.

**Unvalidated.** This comes from a Gemini summary the user pasted on 2026-09-30 of a video by Chris of Acrylic Pixel
Games: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=astNYD6PHSk>. Timestamps point into that video. Neither the video nor the
summary was checked. The last section is the summary's own commentary, not the video's. One dev's experience, one
game, is not evidence of what works.

## The process (the video's)

The speaker treats outreach like B2B lead generation: few, well-qualified leads beat mass mailing.

1. **Find lookalikes** [01:05]. Pick a successful game in your exact subgenre, released in the last 12 months.
2. **Qualify the creators** [06:31]. Search YouTube for that game. Keep a creator only if they play *several* indie
   games (not one game only) and uploaded within the last 3 months.
3. **Collect emails by hand** [08:19]. From each channel's "About" page. YouTube caps this at about 15 a day per
   account, so he uses two accounts for about 30 leads a day, in a spreadsheet.
4. **Write a very short email** [10:20].
   - Subject: `Steam Key: [Game Name] - [Genre]`.
   - Body in the first person ("Hi, I'm Chris, I'm an indie dev"). Publishers can't write this way, so it stands out.
   - One-sentence pitch, store link, press kit link, one proof point (e.g. "5,000 itch.io plays"), and a screenshot or
     capsule image that shows the real gameplay.
5. **Stack social proof** [18:38]. Once a mid-size or large creator has played it, name them in later emails. It
   lowers the next creator's risk.
6. **Don't script the creator** [19:52]. Never ask them to tell viewers to wishlist. A forced ad breaks the
   authenticity, and genuine reactions convert better.
7. **No takers is a signal** [29:30]. If the process is followed and nobody will play it, the game may be the
   problem, not the emails.

## Limits the video admits

- **Needs a recent twin.** [03:41] With a very unusual game, or a spiritual successor to a 15-year-old game, step 1
  breaks: the overlapping creators don't exist.
- **Slow.** [15:13] Creators can take weeks or months to play a key, so they may show an outdated build.
- **Modest result.** [33:09, 35:30] Even with large channels covering it, the game had about 2,157 wishlists, which he
  projected at about $2,500 at launch. He said he was losing money and needed a day job.

## Commentary from the summary (not the video)

- Creator coverage isn't revenue. The viewer → wishlist conversion is low, so YouTube alone is a weak funnel.
- The manual harvesting costs time a solo dev could spend building. The summary suggests key-distribution platforms
  (Keymailer, Woovit) or PR lists instead. *Unvalidated, and each is a dependency to justify before adopting.*
- The "derivative design" charge against step 1 is the summary's reading. The video only says the method doesn't
  apply to unusual games.

## For this project **[C]**

- The method assumes a Steam page and Steam keys. Whether the final product is a Steam game or a browser game isn't
  decided: the user may use web platforms only to seed a genre brand and community before a Steam launch, and wants
  real web numbers first. Until then, a playable link works in the same email in place of a key (cheaper for the
  creator), and the creator list built now carries over to a later Steam launch. *Verify* with a real send.
- It slots into the launch plan's beats: the small-creator email at the web fragment, the demo-to-creators send
  before Next Fest, keys at launch ([04](04-launch-plan.md)).
- Step 2's filter (plays several indie games, active within 3 months) is the useful, cheap part. It applies to any
  list of creators, however the emails are found.
