# Launch plan: the content phase, week by week

Scope: what to do, besides building content, from the day a game leaves volatile systems prototyping to a few weeks
after its Steam launch. Many first games flop because the plan is "I just have to make a good game". This is the
rest of the plan. General, for any game in the 4–8 month cycle ([game-strategy.md](../game-strategy.md) #4).

Written 2026-09-30. Each item says where it comes from:

- **[P]** a marketing summary the user pasted on 2026-09-30. It has no sources: **unvalidated**.
- **[R]** the research in this folder ([02](02-web-lite-funnel.md), [03](03-funding-and-link-rules.md),
  [01](01-reddit-games.md)), which cites its sources.
- **[C]** Claude's suggestion, to fill a gap. Not researched and not decided by the user.
- *verify:* a platform fact from memory, to check on Steamworks before relying on it.

## When the plan starts

The trigger is the end of systems prototyping. From then on, the work is adding content to systems that no longer
change shape. Signs of that point **[C]**:

- The core loop is fun for strangers, not only for you.
- The 300-character pitch is written, and it has a hook ([game-strategy.md](../game-strategy.md) #5).
- New work is mostly "more of X" (areas, creatures, levels), not "what if X worked differently".

Call that day **T0**. Pick a realistic launch week **L**, and plan backwards from it **[P]**. The weeks below assume
T0 = L−26, a 6-month content phase. With a shorter phase, keep the announcement at least 4 months before launch and
compress the middle.

## Rules for the whole period

- **Announce 4–6 months before launch.** Longer campaigns (8–12+ months) tend to leave community channels quiet, and
  interest fades **[P]**. This is about community channels. Wishlists themselves don't go stale: there's "no evidence
  that wishlists 'get old'" **[R, 03]**.
- **2–3 weeks between news beats**, so press and players don't tire of you **[P]**.
- **One call to action per piece.** Every trailer, post and update ends with one clear next step **[P][R, 02]**.
  Before the Steam page exists, it's the mailing list. After, it's the wishlist, with the mailing list second.
- **The mailing list is the channel you own.** It gets higher engagement than any social platform **[P][R, 02]**, and
  it carries over from game to game **[R, 02]**.
- **Few channels.** Don't spread across every network. Discord especially takes time from development **[P]**; it's
  for superfans, when they ask for it **[R, 02]**.
- **No apologies.** Talk about the game with confidence. No self-deprecating lines in posts or newsletters **[P]**.
- **A fixed weekly slot for marketing**, e.g. one day a week, so it neither swallows development nor gets skipped
  **[C]**.

## The timeline

### T0 to L−24: setup and messaging

- **Pitch:** write the elevator pitch and short descriptions. Test them on peers before any public asset **[P]**.
- **Genre pain points:** read reviews of the top-selling and mid-tier games in the genre. List the recurring
  complaints, and answer them in the store description and headlines **[P]**.
- **Positioning:**
  - An underserved genre mix gets "pull PR": press and players are more curious about fresh concepts than about
    crowded genres like platformers **[P]**.
  - Sell mechanics, player benefits and feelings, not lore **[P]**.
- **Steam tags:** choose precise sub-genre tags, so the algorithm files the game with the right neighbours **[P]**.
- **Landing page + mailing list:** a static page with a sign-up and a **lead magnet**, something free in return for
  the email **[P]**. The web fragment is the natural lead magnet **[R, 02]**.
- **Steam Coming Soon page:** pay the $100 app fee **[R, 03]**. *verify:* there's a waiting period between paying and
  releasing, and the page must be public for some weeks before launch.
- **Pick the Next Fest.** It's the last big beat before launch. *verify:* a game gets one Next Fest, and registration
  closes weeks ahead.

### ~L−24: announce

- Make the Steam page public, with:
  - a reveal trailer that shows core gameplay immediately;
  - curated screenshots that show player action and environmental variety **[P]**.
- The main call to action becomes the wishlist **[R, 02]**.

### L−22 to L−16: the web fragment

- Release the free fragment on itch.io (browser) and your own page, a third to halfway through the cycle **[R, 02]**.
  - Not in the same week as the announcement. They're separate beats **[R, 02]**.
  - Only after strangers have played it **[R, 02]**.
- It ends at a wall the player can see, with the call to action on the end screen and the main menu **[R, 02]**.
- Portal builds without the ask, where the portal rules require it **[R, 03]**.
- Email a short list of small streamers and YouTubers in the genre **[R, 02]**.

### L−16 to L−8: grow the list

- **Posting rhythm:** value-first GIFs of one mechanic, dev-insight posts, niche subreddits, sanctioned promotion
  windows. One beat every 2–3 weeks **[P]**; Reddit specifics are unvalidated **[R, 01]**.
- **Closed beta as the hook:** invite people to the mailing list with access to an upcoming closed beta, not with
  "follow the development" **[P]**.
- **The beta itself:** 3–5 days, strictly time-boxed. Sign-ups through a form, invitations by email, access by Steam
  key. The short window makes it urgent, speeds up bug reports and lifts wishlists **[P]**.

### L−8 to L−2: Steam demo and Next Fest

- Put the Steam demo out well before the fest. Early demos earned about 2.5x more wishlists (a weak correlation)
  **[R, 02]**.
- Benchmarks: games entering with under 1,000 wishlists gain a median of 322. With 1,000–9,999, the median gain is
  1,006. About 20% of demo players wishlisting is healthy **[R, 02]**.

**Why Next Fest is the big beat** (a second summary the user pasted on 2026-09-30, **unvalidated [P]**):

- **The biggest burst of attention before launch.** It multiplies wishlists: games that enter with momentum (often
  thousands of wishlists already) can double or triple them in days. *This clashes with the benchmarks above: a
  median gain of about 1,006 for games entering with 1,000–9,999 wishlists means most don't double. Doubling or
  tripling is the good case, not the typical one.*
- **The algorithm rewards momentum.** Fast wishlist growth, many demo downloads and active play in the **first 48
  hours** push the game onto the front page and the "Trending" lists.
- **The demo does the convincing.** A strong demo proves the core loop in one short session.
- **Creators and press hunt for games there.** Coverage brings outside traffic, which feeds the algorithm and the
  festival's rankings.
- **The demo feeds the community.** Players of an early playable version can be funnelled to the mailing list (or
  Discord), so a core of supporters is ready to buy and review on launch day.

So, for this plan: enter with as many wishlists as the earlier beats can gather, have the demo polished before the
fest, send creators the demo ahead of it, and put the mailing list sign-up in the demo **[C]**.

### L: launch

- Email the mailing list. Steam emails every wishlister **[R, 03]**.
- Streamers and YouTubers get keys ahead of launch **[C]**.
- Expect about 0.15x wishlists in first-week sales (median, and 0.10x above $10), with a 10–20x spread between
  games **[R, 03]**.

### After launch

- Keep every fragment online, and point it at the newest game **[R, 02]**.
- The next game's mailing list is this one's **[R, 02]**.
- Kickstarter or Patreon only once people ask how to pay, and only after a Steam page with a few thousand wishlists
  **[R, 03]**.
