# Free web "lite" version to paid full game: how to run the funnel

Scope: how to release free, partial browser versions of a bigger game in progress, and turn the players into support (wishlists, sales, Patreon, Kickstarter). The strategy is adopted; this guide is about doing it well.

Researched 2026-09-30. Sources were fetched on that date unless marked "unverified". Reddit and crowdfunding platform rules are covered in other guides.

## TL;DR

1. Always leave something to buy that the free version visibly lacks. shapez.io, Melvor Idle, The Roottrees are Dead, Friday Night Funkin' and Fallen London all had a clear "more is over there". Lesson from Kittens Game and Candy Box: give everything away and there is nothing left to sell.
2. Do not worry about cannibalisation. No case found shows a free web version hurting sales, and current Steam data says demos help. The old "demos halve sales" claim (EEDAR 2013, console) is correlation only.
3. Browser play is the single biggest lever on free platforms. On itch.io, about 40% of page viewers play a browser game; 6-10% download one.
4. Make the paid version a visible jump: more content, better art, or both. Several big itch.io hits sold modestly on Steam when the paid game was too close to the free one.
5. Put the call to action at the end of the fragment and on the main menu. Portals (CrazyGames) allow store links only in those two places anyway.
6. One call to action per stage. Before a Steam page exists: mailing list. After: wishlist. Discord is for the few superfans, not the default.
7. Keep every free version up forever, and link each old game to the newest one. The cheapest cross-promotion (a link inside your older game) was the best one measured.
8. Release the fragment early in the cycle, not at launch. Separate the fragment from the paid launch by weeks or months.
9. Virality is mostly outside your control. Most documented hits were found by streamers or YouTubers, not pushed by the dev. The fragment's job is to be there and be easy to try.
10. Smallest thing that works: one web build, one end screen with one link, one mailing list. Skip portals, TikTok funnels and multi-platform plans until something shows traction.

## 1. Case studies: what to copy

### Examples to learn from: the free web version fed a paid version

**shapez.io (tobspr, solo at the start).** Free browser version in May 2020, Steam in June 2020. The web version is labelled "shapez Demo" on shapez.io and offers "Try the Steam Version". It was limited: 14 levels, two map markers, one save, no wires layer (per shapez.io's own update notes, found via search, not fetched). GameDiscoverCo's deep dive quotes the developer: "at the launch day you already have 100,000 players or more who playtested it - for free." Steam wishlists at launch were only 958, because "People actually knew the game already from Itch.io." Afterwards the web version kept selling: "the 100-200k monthly users - around 2.500 to 3.000 units are being sold solely through via web version monthly." itch.io itself earned "roughly $8.000". Result: a $1M+ Steam game. https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/deep-dive-how-shapezio-went-from
Note: 2,500-3,000 sales from 100-200k monthly users is roughly 1.5-3% conversion per month. That is the only published web-to-paid rate found.

**Melvor Idle (Brendan Malcolm, solo).** Browser idle game at melvoridle.com. The free version has "a limited number of skills, enemies, and game modes"; the full version is a one-time unlock (Melvor wiki, via search). Later Steam ($9.99) and mobile. Jagex published it from 2021 and reports "more than 1million unique players across all platforms." https://www.jagex.com/news/jagex-announces-partnership-to-publish-melvor-idle , https://wiki.melvoridle.com/w/Full_Version

**The Roottrees are Dead.** Free browser (WebGL) game on itch.io, November 2023, about 8 hours long, AI art. It earned $3,000 in tips in its first month. Rebuilt in Godot with real art and a second chapter, released on Steam January 2025, and "earned over $1 million dollars". The free version stayed up. https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/04/28/how-an-itch-io-game-became-a-million-dollar-hit-the-roottrees-are-dead/

**Friday Night Funkin'.** A Ludum Dare 47 jam prototype (October 2020), then an "expanded demo" on Newgrounds (November 2020). The Kickstarter for "The Full Ass Game" (April 2021) passed its $60,000 goal in hours and raised over $2 million. Warning: as of the Wikipedia fetch the full game is still "Upcoming", five years later. Big crowdfunding plus a free, much-loved demo creates a long, heavy promise. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friday_Night_Funkin%27 (Kickstarter page returned 403, not fetched.)

**Fallen London to Sunless Sea (Failbetter).** Fallen London is a free browser game. Its spin-off Sunless Sea raised £100,803 from 4,271 backers on Kickstarter (2013). About two thirds of pledges came from outside Kickstarter: "our mailing list, Fallen London, social media, press coverage." This is the closest match to "keep one audience across several games." https://www.failbettergames.com/news/sunless-sea-sales-and-funding-deep-dive-part-i-kickstarter

**Cookie Clicker (Orteil).** Free browser game from 2013, still free at cookieclicker.com. Steam version (2021, $4.99, new soundtrack, no ads) reached "over 60,000 concurrent players shortly after release". Eight years of free play did not stop people paying. Orteil also runs a Patreon (earnings unverified). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cookie_Clicker

**Die in the Dungeon.** Brackeys jam game (2021) on itch.io: 1.4M views, 919K browser plays. Steam Early Access in 2025 with 1,694 reviews. The developers: "99% of the marketing was organic, youtubers and streamers just found the game on their own." https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/05/22/more-games-that-made-the-itch-io-to-steam-transition/

**A Dark Room.** Free web game (2013). An iOS port grossed $697,270 in two years. The fetched sales article does not tie iOS sales to the web version, so treat the web version's role as unproven. https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/a-two-year-look-at-the-sales-of-chart-topping-ios-title-i-a-dark-room-i-

### Lessons: what to avoid

**Vampire Survivors.** Released free in the browser on itch.io in March 2021 and "did not see much engagement". Growth came after the $2.99 Steam release, when YouTubers picked it up. Lesson: do not wait for the web version to spread by itself; keep the paid step close and reach out to creators. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vampire_Survivors

**Kittens Game.** A well-known browser idle game since 2014, fully free. It came to Steam in February 2026 at €7.79 and has 75 reviews (94% positive). Lesson: a large free audience that already has everything does not move to a paid copy. https://store.steampowered.com/app/1097410/Kittens_Game/

**Candy Box (aniwey).** A viral free browser game in 2013 that inspired A Dark Room. No paid follow-up by the author was found; the Steam remake "Candy Box U" is by someone else. Lesson: have the link and the mailing list in place before anything spreads. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candy_Box!

**Antimatter Dimensions.** A browser incremental that went to Steam in 2022 as free-to-play, with 91% positive of about 3,400 reviews. Lesson: great reach, but staying free left no paid step. https://store.steampowered.com/app/1399720/Antimatter_Dimensions/

**itch.io hits that sold less than expected.** Zukowski's survey: Tiny Kingdom had 198K itch views but 107 Steam reviews, citing "Poor balance at launch...missing features". Mushroom Musume and Killer Chat! had huge itch numbers and about 1,000 Steam followers each. His conclusion: "Success on itch isn't a guarantee that you will do well on Steam." He also notes (in search results, same article) that one theory for Tiny Islands underperforming was "not enough difference" between the free prototype and the paid game. https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/05/22/more-games-that-made-the-itch-io-to-steam-transition/

### Checked and not relevant

- Getting Over It: Bennett Foddy's homage to Jazzuo's 2002 "Sexy Hiking". A different developer, so not a funnel. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Getting_Over_It_with_Bennett_Foddy
- Townscaper: the web version is a demo released in December 2021, after the full release. It is back-catalogue marketing, not a pre-launch funnel. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Townscaper
- Universal Paperclips: free web, then paid mobile; "over 2 million" players. No sales figures published (search only).
- Krunker: a browser FPS bought by FRVR in 2022 (search only). An exit, not a funnel.
- Wordle, Sandspiel: not checked in depth. Neither was a free-to-paid funnel as far as known (unverified).

## 2. What the experts say: web versions, demos, cannibalisation

**Demos help.** Chris Zukowski, after Steam's 2024 demo update: "demos work" and "they don't hurt your visibility." Demos unlock streamers, festivals and word of mouth. He also suggests launching a free demo on itch.io first to build wishlists before using Steam's one-time demo notification. https://howtomarketagame.com/2024/07/31/what-steams-big-demo-update-means-for-your-marketing-strategy/

**The "demos halve sales" claim is old and weak.** EEDAR (2013, Xbox 360) found games with demos sold about half as much. The press noted it was correlation, not causation. It does not apply to small PC or web games. https://www.gamespot.com/articles/game-demos-can-hurt-sales-suggests-research/1100-6410863/ (search summary; not fetched)

**Keep demos up.** Zukowski's FAQ: keep the demo live long-term, because that raises the chance creators find it. https://howtomarketagame.com/2023/03/14/frequently-asked-question-about-marketing-your-game/

**Browser beats download.** On itch.io, 37-44% of page viewers play a browser game; 6-10% download one. Median lifetime views are 1,582. "You will not make money from Itch.io. Nobody does." It is for cheap testing. https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/05/12/benchmark-itch-io-traffic/

**Don't make the whole game free.** Zukowski argues free games get no Steam algorithm support and price is not the real obstacle: "price is not the biggest obstacle to people buying your game." Switching free to paid resets reviews. So the web version should be a fragment, and the full game should cost money. https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/07/29/dont-make-your-game-free/

**Next Fest numbers.** For games entering with fewer than 1,000 wishlists, the median gain is 322. With 1,000-9,999, it is 1,006. Games that put their demo out well before the fest earned about 2.5x more (a weak correlation). A healthy demo turns about 20% of players into wishlists. https://howtomarketagame.com/2025/03/26/benchmarks-how-many-wishlists-can-i-get-from-steam-next-fest/

**Wishlists to sales.** GameDiscoverCo: the median first-week sales are 0.15x wishlists (0.10x for games over $10), with a 10-20x spread between games. https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/the-state-of-steam-wishlist-conversions

**Lite version vs demo vs prologue.**
- A demo is the start of the game, attached to the Steam page.
- A prologue is a separate free product, "a 30-45 minute core experience" that ends on a cliffhanger with a wishlist call to action. Backbone's prologue earned 95,000 wishlists. Zukowski warns the prologue trick "is probably going away soon" because of Steam's demo changes. https://howtomarketagame.com/2023/04/03/how-to-use-the-prologue-trick-to-potentially-earn-thousands-of-wishlists/
- A "lite" web version, as used by shapez.io and Melvor, is the real game with limits: a level cap, fewer systems, one save. It is played in the browser for hours and linked to the full game.
- A "fragment of a bigger game", as proposed here, is closest to a prologue: a self-contained slice that shows the hook and points to the rest. It works if the missing parts are visible, named and wanted.

## 3. How to frame a fragment

Evidence on wording is thin. No published A/B tests were found. What successful cases did:

- **Say "demo" plainly in the title.** shapez.io's page title is "shapez Demo". Friday Night Funkin' called its Newgrounds build a demo. Melvor calls the paid tier the "Full Version". Nobody hid the fact that more exists.
- **Make the missing parts visible.** shapez.io showed a level cap and locked features. Melvor shows skills you cannot use yet. The Roottrees promised "a whole second chapter". Players should hit the wall and see what is behind it.
- **Put the call to action at the end and on the main menu.** Zukowski's prologue advice: end on a cliffhanger, with the wishlist ask as the credits roll. CrazyGames only allows store links "on main menu or at the end of a demo game", on desktop. https://docs.crazygames.com/requirements/gameplay/
- **One ask at a time.** Zukowski: "Wishlists are for most people, Discord is for super fans, Mailing list is for super duper fans." With a Steam page, the main ask is the wishlist; a mailing list sign-up comes second. https://howtomarketagame.com/2023/03/14/frequently-asked-question-about-marketing-your-game/
- **Mailing lists outperform social links.** "Done right, a mailing list will have the highest engagement rate of any of your platforms (yes even Discord)." One email: 26% open rate, 2% click rate, 1,200+ wishlists. Failbetter used their free browser game to fill the list. https://howtomarketagame.com/2022/06/28/using-mailing-lists-to-gain-thousands-of-wishlists/ . The widely quoted figures (Twitter 0.5-3% click, Discord 18-24%, email 20-34%) appeared in a search summary of the same site but were not found on the fetched page: unverified.
- **Published conversion numbers.** shapez.io: about 1.5-3% of monthly web players bought on Steam each month. Next Fest demos: a healthy rate is about 20% of players wishlisting. Nothing else solid was found.

For Patreon and Kickstarter, the pattern is the same: the free fragment builds the mailing list, and the list funds the campaign. Two thirds of Sunless Sea's money came from its own channels. Link rules for those platforms are in the crowdfunding guide.

## 5. Timing in a 4-8 month cycle and keeping one audience

**When to release the fragment.**
- Zukowski: "don't launch your demo until you have had strangers play it", and do not launch the demo at the same time as the announcement; they are separate beats. Put demos out months before a big event: early demos earned about 2.5x more at Next Fest.
- For a 4-8 month cycle, a practical reading: release the fragment around one third to halfway through, once the core loop is fun. That leaves time to learn from it, and weeks between the fragment and the paid launch.
- shapez.io is the exception: web and Steam launched a month apart and it still worked, because the web version kept selling afterwards.

**How many.** No evidence supports several fragments per game. The successful cases had one free version that they updated. Default to one fragment per game, updated when there is real news.

**Keeping one audience across games.**
- Use one studio identity, not one account per game. Zukowski: build under one name so each release adds to the same audience.
- Link old games to the new one. Butterscotch Shenanigans on Crashlands 2: "the most effective two things we did by far were also the cheapest and easiest: just linking to Crashlands 2 from the Crashlands store page and from within Crashlands itself." Zukowski: "Keep those demos up forever." He also suggests a link that updates remotely so old builds always point to the newest game. https://howtomarketagame.com/2024/08/26/how-to-cross-promote-under-the-new-steam-rules/
- For a static web build, that remote link can be a plain JSON or text file on your own site, which the end screen reads (or a fixed URL on your site that you redirect). No server needed.
- The mailing list is the one channel you own across every game. Failbetter's free game fed the list that funded the next games.
- Warning for short cycles: Failbetter's audience came from one world (Fallen London). Zukowski notes the more similar the game, the better the click-through. Unrelated genres every few months will not carry an audience as well.

## Main risks to design around

- Giving away too much: keep a visible wall at the end of every fragment (Kittens Game, Candy Box).
- A paid game too close to the free one: plan the jump in content or art from the start (Tiny Islands theory, Tiny Kingdom).
- Waiting for virality: ship the link, the list and creator outreach with the fragment (Vampire Survivors on itch).
- Unrelated games each cycle: audiences carried best within one world or series (Failbetter, Crashlands). A shared world, style or genre across the 4-8 month games helps the back catalogue work.
- Crowdfunding too big a promise: Friday Night Funkin' raised $2M+ and is still unreleased five years on. Scope any campaign to what one cycle can deliver.

## Hypotheses to put a number on

*Unvalidated. The user's, 2026-09-30, from a conversation with their son (a pre-teen's opinion, nothing more).*

- **Web-portal players mostly don't remember or follow.** Players aged about 10 to 17 play on web platforms to switch
  off, and forget the game afterwards. If true, the conversion from portal play to a mailing list, a community or a
  wishlist is very low. The plan needs a measured figure for this, not the assumption.
- **Familiarity is a payoff of its own.** Much advertising isn't meant to convert at once. It makes the name familiar,
  so the next sighting feels trusted. If web play works like this, the return shows up later, at the Steam launch, and
  a conversion count on the day of play undercounts it.

The two pull apart: measuring only direct conversion would kill a brand-awareness effect that can't be seen that way.
Deciding what counts as "viable" for the web direction has to allow for both: web as the product, or web as a seed for
a later Steam launch.
