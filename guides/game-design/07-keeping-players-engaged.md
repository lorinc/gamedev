# 07 · Keeping players engaged (without being evil)

Scope: what makes players want to keep playing a game to the end, and what makes them drop it. General, for any game.

**Source.** The full transcript of Mark Brown's Game Maker's Toolkit video on engagement, pasted by the user on
2026-09-30: <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=hbzGO_Qonu0>. The user says it is where the idea of "games I never
finished" came from. The transcript has no timestamps. It is one designer's opinion from his own play (he says "the
factors that I've found really work on me"), not research, and he says different things work for different players.
Everything below is from the transcript unless marked. It pairs with [06 gameplay loops](06-gameplay-loops.md).

## The question and the ground rule

Mark Brown never finished Doom, which he loves, yet finished games he likes less. He asks how games keep players
engaged.

- **Engaging, not addictive.** He rules out games built so players can't stop themselves, and won't discuss Skinner
  boxes, daily rewards, resource decay, loss aversion and the like. He says responsible designers shouldn't craft
  those. The video covers fun things that make you want to keep playing "without completely hijacking your brain".

## The factors

### Pacing: swap pillars, vary intensity

- A game has several **pillars** (Uncharted: combat, climbing, puzzles, set-pieces, cutscenes). Naughty Dog swaps
  between them constantly, so when the player tires of one, the game has already moved on.
- Each pillar also has an **intensity** (a puzzle is calmer than a firefight). Move fluidly between intensities: too
  long on calm is boring, too long at maximum leads to exhaustion or desensitisation. He traces Uncharted 2
  alternating calm and intense stretches, with the calmest section (the Tibetan village) just before the biggest
  siege.
- Tight control is easy in a linear game. In an open game, the substitute is many types of activity, so players can
  regulate their own fun when they get bored.

### Novelty

- New areas, mechanics and enemy types keep attention. Mario is mostly platforming, yet every stage brings a new way
  to play, so you never know what to expect.
- Each new thing is an exciting hit that **burns out quickly**, and the player needs another soon.

### Anticipation: tease what's coming

- Mystery and foreshadowing excite the player *before* the new content arrives, which stretches the time between
  drops. He calls it economical.
- Examples: the impossible puzzle door near the start of The Witness; Sen's Fortress in Dark Souls; Hollow Knight
  promising new powers and showing barriers you can't yet pass without saying what the powers are.
- Narrative mystery works the same way (cliffhangers, unanswered questions) but few games manage it. His example is
  Firewatch, which keeps you going to learn how it ends.

### Goals: a long-term goal with short-term ones on the way

His example is Stardew Valley. The implicit long-term goal is turning a messy patch of land into a great farm, and
the dream carries many hours of repetitive work. Why it works:

- **Self-expression:** you build the farm you like and set your own goals.
- **Short-term goals on the path:** the community centre, new buildings, house upgrades.
- **Planning:** crops, animals, seasons and partners are long-term strategic choices, so you aren't "trudging
  mindlessly".
- **Positive feedback loop:** money buys seeds and better tools, which make more money. Same in Monster Hunter
  (loot monsters, upgrade the character).
- **Optimising a system:** hand-watering becomes sprinklers; in Factorio, hand-mined resources become machines, then
  machines run on power that doesn't run out. "The desire to create an optimised system that works on its own is
  very potent" (he guesses it appeals to programmers).
- **Fantasising about the future:** a skill tree is looked at for the day the player can use all of it on what once
  felt hard.

### Compelling challenge

- Flow comes from a challenge neither too easy (boring) nor too hard (stressful). Resident Evil 4 uses dynamic
  difficulty; most games need a tuned, playtested difficulty curve.
- **Failure isn't bad.** Tetris and Spelunky are full of failure, and players come back if runs are **relatively
  short**, if they **feel they improve each time**, and if they **know the next session will be markedly
  different** (often random generation).
- Challenge can be problem-solving, spatial awareness or decision-making, not only reflexes.

## His own diagnosis

He can't put down games full of novel experiences teased through mystery and anticipation (Dark Souls, Metroid, The
Witness). He dropped Doom because of long stretches of high-intensity combat (exhausting) and little novelty next to
Titanfall 2. Others may need competition or rewards to show off.

## Possible uses **[C]**

- Use the factors as a checklist of reasons a game gets dropped: one intensity for too long, nothing new, nothing
  teased, no goal within reach, no sense of getting better.
- Several factors are what the idle genre already runs on: the Stardew and Factorio items (a feedback loop, automating
  your own work, looking forward to a later state) are accumulation and investment. The ones he excludes (daily
  rewards, resource decay, loss aversion) are also common in that genre, so what he excludes and what he praises
  split the genre's toolbox.
- The "short runs, visible improvement, next session different" line is the closest thing in the video to "finishable
  and replayable differently". It is about roguelike-style runs with random generation.
- Anticipation is the only factor on the list that costs almost no content: a thing the player can see and can't yet
  reach.
