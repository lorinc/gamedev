# 06 · Gameplay loops: core, medium, meta

Scope: how loops of different lengths keep players playing and coming back. General, for any game.

**Source.** The full transcript of a video on gameplay loops, pasted by the user on 2026-09-30 because they didn't
trust the Gemini summaries. The video's title and channel weren't recorded. The link in the Gemini summary is
<https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Sk-nbAtIUko>, not checked against the transcript. The transcript has no timestamps.
It is one designer's opinion, argued from examples (Hades, Doom, Mario, Steamworld Dig 2, Pikmin and others). Nothing
in it is measured. Everything below is from the transcript unless marked. Where the Gemini summaries differ, see the
last section.

## The video's claims

### What a loop is

A gameplay loop is a series of repeated actions or emotional states that is the foundation of everything the player
does. Most games run several loops of different lengths at once, some as short as seconds. Hades: start with basic
abilities, collect power-ups, fight tougher enemies, die, look at what you unlocked, go again. New weapons and god
powers are added to each run while the structure stays fixed, so it stays fresh and the player still builds mastery
of the underlying systems.

### Short loops: fun now

- **Core loops** are the quick, mechanically intense loops the player spends most of their time in. Doom: enter a
  room, kill everything, grab the drops, move on, several times a minute.
- They must feel good to use, because the player repeats them hundreds of times in one session.
- Mario's platforming challenge (approach, execution, recovery) is one. Nintendo works hard at making moving around
  fun, because that is 99% of play. The challenges are layers over the same loop.
- **Copying the structure without the core fails.** The video says the Mario clones of the day copied the larger
  loops and the overall structure, but didn't make running and jumping fun.
- The ideal core loop is one the player rarely thinks about (the best shmups, great action games). It is the constant
  foundation the game returns to after the bigger stuff.

### Long loops: a reason to return

- Total War's conquest cycle (pick a target, muster, fight, hold or retreat) takes hours, so a session holds one or
  two. It is disconnected from the moment-to-moment play (menus, orders).
- Long loops offer an abstract motivation, not immediate fun. Old-school JRPGs have a dull core loop (single combat
  turns), but players who love the meta loop (story and character growth over tens of hours) stay for it. The wish
  to see the loop resolve brings them back after the thrill of the gameplay fades.

### The two-sided problem

- Mostly short loops: fun in the moment, but no reason to come back.
- Mostly long loops: satisfaction is delayed, a lot of effort comes before the fun, and the game joins the "I'll get
  around to it" pile.
- **The fix.** The "endlessly playable" games build their whole structure around **one or two medium-length loops**,
  supported by longer and shorter ones. That gives a predictable rhythm and short-term goals that point to where the
  fun is.

### Medium loops need anchors

- Towers and bases to capture, overused as they are, anchor the medium loop of "explore an area, conquer it, move
  on". Without shrines in Breath of the Wild or cult outposts in Far Cry, large stretches would have nothing to do
  but travel to the next story segment, and finding a consistent groove would be hard.
- The eight themed areas of a Pokémon game work the same way. The speaker's view: people say they want total freedom,
  but an open-world Pokémon would more often leave them lost, confused and bored before they finish. Eight gyms, each
  holding a number of rounds of the core battle loop, and a capping boss fight keep players from feeling overwhelmed.

### Worked examples

- **Steamworld Dig 2: two feeding loops.** Mine deeper until the pack is full or the torch runs out, then return to the
  surface, leaving some loot or an unexplored part behind. Sell the haul and buy upgrades. The balance is such that
  you never *quite* have enough to buy everything, which gives a new goal and sends you back down for the abandoned
  loot. The loop runs about every 10 minutes, with an occasional optional puzzle dungeon. A zone takes about an hour.
- **Session length.** Pick the ideal session length first. A session should last about one big meta loop and hold a
  few rounds of the medium loop, so each session feels complete and there is always something new on return.
- **Pikmin: an imposed schedule.** Each day: morning to rebuild forces after yesterday's losses, afternoon to push into
  new terrain, fight bosses and find loot, evening to rush everything back to the ship before night predators eat
  what's left outside. A one-to-two-hour session holds about five or six days, about enough to finish or fully explore
  one area.
- **Factorio: milestones.** Sessions of a few hours, paced by technology milestones. Reaching the next research type
  takes about a session. Smaller goals (other techs, production expansions, alien-killing missions) keep you busy on
  the way.
- **Devil Daggers: a loop inside one minute or two.** The run has distinct phases (squid towers; first upgrade and
  first spider; second upgrade and first centipede…). They ground a chaotic game with an intense core loop and make
  progress and self-set goals easier.

### The dark side

- Loops can turn a merely bad game into an exploitative one. Candy Crush has a flashy, satisfying core loop, bars to
  fill, levels to beat, and arbitrary milestones to bring you back. Bejeweled uses the same tactics in a good game;
  the difference is the motive: Candy Crush cuts loops short on purpose and leaves you unsatisfied until you pay.
- Destiny 2 (the speaker's opinion, and he says so): a very satisfying shooter loop, and a steady string of small
  upgrades and milestones that make you feel you are progressing "when you kind of… aren't". He played the whole
  campaign and some DLC before noticing he wasn't having fun.
- His test for players: step back and ask whether you're having fun or just going through the loop's motions.

### Exceptions

Her Story works because it has no rhythm (the lack of one leads you to make assumptions about the mystery). AI War
never makes you feel like a conqueror. The video's point: loops are a powerful tool, not a law.

## Possible uses **[C]**

- The video gives a vocabulary and a checklist, not numbers. Its sharpest tests are these: does the core feel good with
  nothing on top; is there one identifiable medium loop; how long is it; how many rounds fit a session; what pulls
  the player back tomorrow.
- The medium loop in the examples always has a *limit* that forces a turn (full pack, night falling, a research
  gate). A medium loop without a forcing limit is worth checking for.
- Session length is a design input, chosen first.
- The dark-side section gives a cheap self-test for a game that uses progression bars: is the progress real, or
  does it only feel like progress.

- **Invented by Gemini:** the "checklist fatigue" warning and the call for "systemic loops" (weather, factions); the

  not loot drops); "forcing the player to clear 50 outposts instead of 10"; the wayfinding and urban-planning
  analogy; the "45-minute session you can picture without UI" idea. These are the summary's own commentary, not the
  video's. Don't quote them as the video.

  stones are "combat, expanding production lines" (the video lists techs, production expansions, alien-killing

- **Open-world framing.** The second summary's "Open World Application" and "Risk Assessment" sections are Gemini's,
  probably from the prompt. The video is about games in general; its only open-world points are the anchors
  (Breath of the Wild, Far Cry) and the open-world Pokémon thought experiment.
- **Missing from the first summary, present in the second and in the video:** Steamworld Dig 2, Pikmin, Factorio and
  the two-sided problem. The second summary is the more faithful one on these.
