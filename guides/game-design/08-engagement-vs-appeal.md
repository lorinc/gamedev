# 08 · Engagement versus appeal

Scope: a game must do two different jobs, make people want to look at it and make them want to keep playing. General,
for any game.

**Source.** "Your Game Must Do These 2 Things", Indie Game Clinic, <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rTxKsBclbxk>
(source supplied by the user on 2026-10-01). The transcript was pasted by the user on 2026-09-30 and has no
timestamps. It is one developer-educator's opinion from games he sees on his channel; "80% of the
games I see" is his impression, not a count. He says the split isn't a textbook definition and some things fall
outside it. Much of it overlaps earlier guides, noted below. Everything here is from the transcript unless marked.

## The split

- **Engagement** is ongoing: what makes a player keep playing once they've started. It lives in the game design and
  the feel.
- **Appeal** is whether anyone looks at the game, downloads the demo, or thinks it's their kind of thing.
- They overlap but come apart. Some games are very engaging with no distinctive appeal; some have a striking appeal
  and aren't engaging once played.

## Engagement: two common failures

1. **A string of challenges with no interesting decisions or problem solving.**
   - *Toys versus games.* A toy is inherently fun to handle (a sword that sounds good, animates well, shakes the
     screen). A good action game is a set of such toys strung together with challenges, goals and rewards. The
     moment-to-moment feel is only as good as the toys added up. Coins, chests, and a sword each have to feel good on
     their own.
   - **Order of work:** build one vertical slice, one level or one scene, and make it feel right before making content
     (story, levels, enemy and weapon variety). "Don't make 10 weapons for your FPS if the first weapon isn't fun."
     The same holds for a cozy game: talking to a character should feel good (Animal Crossing's sounds and turns).
   - *Harder isn't better.* Giving the enemy more health or the player less time appeals to a small hard-core niche.
     Most players want interesting or difficult decisions in their head.
   - *The "but" test.* Describe the game in a sentence; if there's a "but" in it, there are competing goals. Bad
     North: I earn money for upgrades only by saving the houses, *but* saving them means leaving my generals' safe
     hill. Quote used: Sid Meier, a game is a series of interesting decisions (the speaker allows exceptions). Even a
     twitchy game needs split-second choices (play safe, or risk more coins).
2. **A world with no activity.** Starting from the environment: a scene in an engine with a camera and things in it.
   It feels like a game because it's in an engine, but without an activity it isn't one. People work for long periods
   adding beautiful, immersive interactions that add no gameplay. Players don't play just to be immersed; they have to
   be doing something. The same goes for pure challenge: people want to survive when the rest of play is rewarding in
   itself.

## Appeal

- Part of it is the design (a mechanical hook such as Portal's portals), but a hook alone rarely makes a game stand
  out. The rest is creative direction: art style, premise, theme.
- **Mix flavours.** Animal Well: well-designed lateral-thinking puzzles (engagement), plus cute and dark and
  mysterious at once (appeal). Puzzle platformers are "a cursed genre", but he calls the "this genre doesn't do well
  on Steam" talk tea-leaf reading: it doesn't say why particular games did or didn't succeed.
- **The central fantasy.** What does the player get to do that they might like to do in real life? Pacific Drive:
  looking after a cool, personified car in a stalker-like zone. A distinct fantasy can also shrink the work: crafting
  only for the car means nothing in the world needs to be harvestable. The theme suggests the mechanics.
- **The funnel.** Creative direction is the wide end ("people who like Vikings will like this"); once they're
  curious, engagement must hold. Look for a new angle on a familiar genre as an entry point for people outside it.
- **Think of the trailer and the 15-second clip:** art style, theme and fantasy are what they show.
- **Art needn't be artistic skill.** Clever asset packs, or a minimalist style borrowed from something that already
  exists: Mini Metro uses subway-map graphic design, so there were no characters or real locations to draw, and the
  look reminds people of the theme.
- **Look outside games** for themes and visuals. Pacific Drive draws on 80s and 90s TV (Knight Rider, Back to the
  Future, Ghostbusters).
- **Test appeal early.** Show concept art, characters and animations from the start, before the game is done; a
  "take my money" reaction signals a market. A character from a monster-battling game in progress (an acorn-cat)
  drew enthusiasm from his Discord. Appeal is far easier to test than engagement, which needs organised playtests.
  Games made "for no one" are released into a void.
- He also argues programmers should partner with people who are better at story, art and creative direction. Not
  taken further here: it assumes a team.

## Possible uses **[C]**

- *Overlaps with earlier guides:* toys and feel is [02 game feel](02-game-feel.md) and the core-loop test in
  [06](06-gameplay-loops.md); interesting decisions is in [01](01-foundations.md); order of work is
  [05](05-process-and-scope.md). The new parts are the engagement/appeal split, the central fantasy and the funnel, and
  testing appeal separately and early.
- The "world with no activity" failure is worth checking any living-system game against: what is the player *doing*
  that is a decision, while the system around them changes.
- The two jobs could be measured separately in a web test: the share of people who start the game from its thumbnail
  and title (appeal) against how long they stay and whether they return (engagement). Not from the video.
- Appeal can be tested before anything is built, with images and GIFs.
