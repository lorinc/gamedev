# 18 · What makes a good deck-building roguelike

Scope: one player's view of what separates good deck-building roguelikes from clones. General, for any game with a
collected set of abilities used in turn-based encounters.

**Source.** "What Makes A Good DECK BUILDING Roguelike?", PepperHead, <https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UG_T3S6-qUw>.
The transcript (auto-captions, with timestamps) was pasted by the user on 2026-10-05. It is one player's opinion,
after "countless hours" in over 30 deck-building roguelikes; he speaks as a player, not a developer, and nothing in it
is measured. The captions garble names; spellings below are corrected where the game is certain, and marked where not.
Everything outside the last section is from the video.

## The claims

### 1. A unique mechanic matters more here than in action roguelikes [1:39–6:07]

- Action roguelikes feel different from each other within seconds, even when their ideas are alike (Rogue Legacy and
  Dungreed): real-time play and physics make jumping, dashing and attacking feel unique.
- Deck-building roguelikes mostly draw on one source, Slay the Spire (2017), which was inspired by Dream Quest (mobile,
  2014), the first of the kind. Later games (Monster Train, Roguebook, Across the Obelisk) keep its frame and add a
  twist: Roguebook has two characters and positioning, Monster Train three boards resolved like an auto-battler,
  Across the Obelisk four characters with separate turns (longer, more strategic fights).
- Many cards repeat across games, especially early: low-cost block, heal, small damage. With no physics or real-time
  play, the same effect feels the same. The play is text: "games could ultimately be played blindfolded if given the
  necessary information."
- So the genre is narrow for original play, and a twist is more important than in action games. "A deck building game
  that has no unique mechanic but just a huge variety of cards or passive effects would simply be a Slay the Spire
  clone, no matter how different the story, the visuals or the audio is." His first question on any new one: what is
  the gimmick?
- A twist also lets cards be designed around it. **Arcanium's Cleave** is practically identical to Slay the Spire's
  (1 energy, small damage to several enemies), yet three characters and board positioning make it feel different.
- **Deck of Ashes:** spending health to bring cards back from the discard pile wasn't impactful enough for him; despite
  RPG elements, characters and exploration, it played too much like Slay the Spire and felt dull.
- Slay the Spire is one of the simplest of them: being first, it didn't need a twist to stand out.
- The genre is only a few years old, and new approaches keep appearing: Balatro (poker), Peglin (Peggle).
- Indie games can't lean on fluid play or satisfying combat visuals, as action games can, so a twist matters more.

### 2. Out-of-combat play: the other 20% [6:07–8:49]

- Most of these games split into combat and the time between fights; over 80% of the content is usually in combat.
- The time between links encounters, adds quick decision points (usually to upgrade the deck), and is a breather.
- **Wildfrost:** after a fight, either the Muncher (delete two cards for good, so draws are more consistent) or the
  Frozen Travelers (one of three new companions). Either is viable, depending on the build.
- **Shops** let players add or change cards without risk: welcoming in a challenging genre.
- **Random events** are his favourite: a risk-versus-reward decision, often unique to the game, adding immersion, and
  impactful "with just a simple click". Events unknown until entered add to the risk, because the player can't tell
  whether they're worth it.
- **Monster Train** (the captions say "the Malika mutator" event): take up to three items that only add negative
  effects; survive two battles holding them and trade them for powerful ones. Taking none, one, two or three each
  changes the run: "great design".
- Any event keeps players engaged with simple but interesting decisions across a run.

### 3. Card design [8:49–19:36]

**Categories and rarity**

- Cards are sorted into categories that follow the game's mechanics (Slay the Spire: attack, skill, power; Monster
  Train: units and spells). This keeps a deck organised instead of a pile of random cards.
- Rarity keeps a sense of progression through a run, instead of everything being available from the start.
- **Rares are stronger, but more situational,** so cards of every rarity can matter, not only the rares carrying the
  run. **Grand Finale** (rare): 50 damage to all enemies, but only with an empty draw pile. Strong, but the condition
  keeps it from being overpowered; it would be wrong as a common.
- **Commons are simple and combine easily.** **Sword Boomerang**: 3 damage to a random enemy, three times, for 1
  energy. Weak alone, but each +1 strength counts three times. **Bludgeon** (rare, 32 damage, 3 energy) looks
  flashier to new players but combines with little, so it can be worse than Sword Boomerang.
- **Commons carry the early game** even where rares are much stronger (Astrea's dice). In Balatro, Gluttonous Joker
  (common, +4 mult per club) is strictly weaker than Onyx Agate (+8 per club), but it shows up earlier and costs less,
  so it gets the player through the first encounters until the rares that win the run appear.
- **Wildfrost has no rarity at all.** He likes the game, but counts this among its biggest flaws: runs are too
  inconsistent and random in power, every card is equally likely at all times, so specific builds are hard to aim for
  and it can frustrate.
- His balance: simple commons that combine, plus a few powerful but situational cards, giving distinct paths to
  different builds.

**Fun over balance**

- The most important thing is whether a card is fun. "One of the best compliments a deck building game can receive
  is how satisfying it is to play." The best moments he has seen are combinations working "hand in hand".
- The best-designed game is not always the most satisfying. Cards should combine, and players should build their own
  combos.
- **Balatro:** he finds its balance uneven, but it gives constant chances to chain cards and jokers into huge
  results, and it is one of the easiest to understand. Most of the genre is full of rules and keywords, which
  intimidates newcomers; Balatro is clear even to players who don't know poker.
- "Beating an encounter with ease thanks to a combination of cards and items collected and chosen by the player is one
  of the best feelings playing these games."
- **Foreign Influence** (Watcher): choose a card from another class. Not strong, rarely a viable strategy, but he's
  always excited to take it, for the unusual situations and chance combos.
- **Judgment** (Watcher, rare): kill any enemy at or under 30 health. It combines with nothing and is about as strong
  in every build, so it's boring, even if powerful.

**Controlled randomness**

- Choosing from three random options is "a very enjoyable feeling". **Hearthstone's Discover** (pick one of three
  random cards) lets the player be strategic with randomness: matches stay consistent but each one is unique.
- **Astrea:** every turn rolls several dice with very different faces, but the game is designed so that nearly every
  face, even weak or harmful-looking ones, can be used somehow.
- **Random-target cards** like Sword Boomerang are best used by first reducing the number of enemies, or bringing
  health to amounts that make the random split favourable. **Monster Train's** "Excavated Eruption" (caption spelling
  uncertain) rewards it: extra energy if the card kills.
- "The entire point of controlled randomness is the satisfaction of feeling as if players outsmarted a game by
  controlling aspects that may at first seem out of their control."
- Not a necessity, one fun concept among many. Cards that can combine in many situations, or that create a unique
  effect, are the most likely to be exciting.

### 4. Themes and visuals [19:36–23:08]

- Deck-building is about the play, not cards. The Steam tag now holds many games without cards: collect items, units
  or abilities during a run and use them against enemies, usually turn-based. Astrea uses dice, Peglin orbs, Backpack
  Hero items placed in a backpack.
- That freedom lets developers put more into theme, aesthetics and visuals.
- These games are slow: players spend turn after turn on one static screen against one encounter, so visuals that
  carry the theme and mood matter, so that it doesn't feel like "staring at numbers".
- **Peglin:** minimal art, but each floor looks clearly different in and out of combat: a sense of adventure.
  **Astrea:** he likes the art, but the biomes are colour-coded so alike that he doesn't feel he is progressing.
- Not a demand for outstanding visuals, as in an exploration game like a metroidvania, but for enough immersion.
- **Roguebook:** its theme, world, music and animation make him want another run, knowing they will keep him immersed
  while he ponders complicated turns. Slay the Spire is nothing revolutionary visually, but its ambience fits.
